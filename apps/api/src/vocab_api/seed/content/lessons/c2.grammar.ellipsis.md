# Ellipsis & substitution

**Ellipsis** is leaving words out when the listener can easily fill them back
in. **Substitution** is replacing a repeated phrase with a short pro-form
(*do, so, one, that*). Fluent speech is full of both — and so is every native
conversation.

## Auxiliary ellipsis

In replies and follow-up clauses the verb phrase can collapse to just the
auxiliary:

- *Can you help? — **I can.*** (not *I can help*)
- *She speaks French, and her brother **does** too.* (substitution with *does*)

## Ellipsis in short answers

- *Who broke it? — **I did.***
- *Will they come? — **I hope so.*** (substitution with *so*)

## Substituting noun phrases

- *I need a laptop. Do you have **one**?* (*one* = *a laptop*)
- *The meeting at ten is better than **the one** at twelve.*

## Register

- **Speech** allows aggressive ellipsis: *Coming? — Sure.*
- **Formal writing** avoids it: do not drop subjects or auxiliaries in a report.

## Common mistakes

| Correct | Common mistake |
|---|---|
| I hope **so**. | I hope it. |
| She likes tea, and he **does** too. | She likes tea, and he likes too. |
| Do you have **one**? | Do you have it? (when it was not previously specific) |

## Quick check

- *Will it rain? — I think ___ .* → **so**
- *He runs, and she ___ too.* → **does**
- *I lost my keys. Have you seen ___?* → **them** (or *any*)
