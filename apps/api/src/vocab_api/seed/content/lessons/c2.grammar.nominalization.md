# Nominalization

**Nominalization** is the habit of expressing an action as a **noun** instead
of a **verb**: *implement → implementation, fail → failure, decide → decision*.
It is the engine of formal and academic English — and a common source of
wordy, soulless prose when overused.

## The transformation

| Verb-based | Nominalized |
|---|---|
| We **implemented** the policy. | The **implementation** of the policy … |
| The system **failed**. | The system's **failure** … |
| They **decided** to expand. | Their **decision** to expand … |

Notice the shift: a whole clause becomes a single noun phrase. The sentence now
needs a new verb to carry the tense.

## Denser noun groups

Nominalization lets you stack modifiers:

- *The **rapid implementation** of the **revised pricing policy*** — one noun
  phrase where five verbs might have stood.

## When it helps

- **Formal register**: reports, contracts, academic prose.
- **Backgrounding**: *The **introduction** of the policy caused debate.*
  (focus on the effect, not the actor)

## When it hurts

Over-nominalization hides who did what and buries the meaning:

- Weak: *The undertaking of the investigation was conducted with the aim of the
  establishment of the facts.*
- Strong: *We investigated the facts.*

The best writers nominalize **deliberately**, not by default.

## Common mistakes

| Correct | Common mistake |
|---|---|
| the **failure** of the launch | the failing of the launch |
| Our **decision** to invest | Our deciding to invest |
| *Clear*: We reviewed the data. | We conducted a review of the data (if needless) |

## Quick check

- *They ___ (analyze) the samples.* → the **analysis**
- *The ___ (reject) of the proposal surprised us.* → **rejection**
- Write the nominal form of *to refuse*: → **refusal**
